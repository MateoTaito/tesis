How Social Memory, Audit Strategies, and Institutional Memory Interact to Shape Tax Compliance Dynamics
Introduction

Tax evasion is a complex, emergent phenomenon that arises from the interaction of heterogeneous taxpayers making boundedly rational decisions under uncertainty, enforcement agencies with limited resources, and evolving social and institutional environments. Agent-based models (ABMs) are particularly well-suited to study this complexity because they can capture heterogeneity, social interaction, learning dynamics, and institutional feedback loops that traditional analytical models cannot [7]. Below, I synthesize findings from the agent-based modeling and behavioral tax compliance literatures, organized around the three interacting mechanisms in your proposed model.
1. Social Memory and Peer Effects in Tax Compliance

A central finding across multiple ABMs is that taxpayers do not make compliance decisions in isolation; rather, they are strongly influenced by the observed behavior and audit outcomes of their social contacts.

Network effects on compliance dynamics. Andrei, Comer, and Koehler [1] demonstrate that network topology has a statistically significant effect on aggregate tax compliance. Using seven different network structures (no network, von Neumann, Moore, Ringworld, Erdős–Rényi, Small Worlds, and Power Law), they show that networks with higher closeness centrality—such as Power Law and Erdős–Rényi networks—allow information about audits and penalties to propagate rapidly, leading to higher mean gross tax rates. Crucially, targeting highly interconnected individuals ("big fish") for audit resulted in a statistically lower mean gross tax rate than targeting disconnected individuals, because the honest behavior of hubs propagated through the network via imitating agents [1].

Imitation and social learning. Hokamp and Pickhardt [3] introduce "imitating b-type agents" who observe the success of tax evasion within their social network (of size ν = 4) and copy successful evasion behavior. If the average net income of evaders in their social network exceeds that of honest taxpayers, these agents conceal their tax liabilities based on the average cheating observed. Conversely, if they or their network contacts are audited and penalized, they "mutate" to honest behavior for a fixed number of periods (τ = 4) [3]. This creates a dynamic social memory: agents remember and respond to their own audit outcomes and those of their peers, but the memory decays over time.

Contagion dynamics. Di Mauro, Pluchino, and Biondo [4] model tax evasion as a contagion game on a small-world network, where agents decide their compliance based on personal satisfaction from public goods and imitation of neighbors. They find a critical threshold in the initial percentage of taxpayers (around 55% in their parameterization): above this threshold, the population converges to a high-compliance equilibrium; below it, evasion becomes the dominant strategy. This threshold depends on both the "Imitation Factor" (sensitivity to neighbor behavior) and the "Capital Factor" (sensitivity to one's own economic outcomes), illustrating how social memory and personal experience interact [4].

Empirical evidence for peer effects. Alm, Bloomquist, and McKee [10] use laboratory experiments to demonstrate that information about neighbors' filing and reporting behavior has statistically significant and economically large effects on individual compliance. Providing information about the percentage of neighbors who file taxes can either increase or decrease compliance depending on the specific content of that information. Informing taxpayers about audit results (penalties paid by audited neighbors) increased both filing and reporting rates, suggesting that visible enforcement creates positive spillover effects through social channels [10].
2. Audit Strategies: Random, Risk-Based, and Income-Targeted

The literature consistently shows that the choice of audit strategy dramatically shapes compliance dynamics, especially when taxpayers are socially connected and adapt their behavior over time.

Random vs. risk-based auditing. Hashimzade, Myles, and Rablen [5] compare random audits with predictive-analytics-based targeting (using Tobit and logit models trained on past audit data) in a rich ABM with occupational choice, social networks, and endogenous belief formation. They find that predictive analytics significantly outperforms random audits in terms of revenue: targeting the largest evaders (level-targeting) or the most likely evaders (rate-targeting) both yield first-order stochastic dominance over random audits. The authors show that predictive analytics effectively implements an income cutoff rule—almost all audit effort concentrates on the lowest declared incomes—which mirrors theoretical results on optimal auditing with commitment [5].

Audit strategies across occupations. Hashimzade, Myles, Page, and Rablen [7] compare four audit strategies in an ABM: (1) fixed probability of audit for all self-employed, (2) fixed number of audits per occupation each period, (3) switching audits between occupations, and (4) randomly switching audits between occupations. The fixed-number-per-occupation strategy dominates all others in terms of revenue, measured by first-order stochastic dominance. The authors attribute this to a non-linearity (or hysteresis) in the response of tax revenue to changes in beliefs and compliance: constancy in audit pressure on each occupation is beneficial because the deterioration in compliance from reduced auditing in some periods outweighs the gains from increased auditing in others [7].

Risk-based audits in behavioral models. Hashimzade and Myles [6] further demonstrate that predictive analytics (using Tobit regression based on declared income, prior audit history, and occupation) increases compliance and revenue compared to random audits. The authors find that the audit rule implied by predictive analytics closely approximates a cutoff rule based on declared income: almost all audited taxpayers are in the lowest percentiles of declared income. Importantly, the behavioral response to audits depends on the "target effect" (where audited taxpayers update their subjective audit probability upward) rather than the "bomb-crater effect" (where audits reduce perceived risk), a distinction with major implications for modeling institutional memory [6].

Income-targeted auditing in agent-based models. Andrei et al. [1] test a "Big Fish" strategy on Power Law networks, where highly connected hubs are audited with 100% probability. This strategy yields a statistically significant lower mean gross tax rate than auditing disconnected individuals—initially counterintuitive, but explained by the fact that fewer penalties are collected when the population is more honest. This demonstrates that audit targeting strategies must be evaluated not just by direct revenue but by their indirect effects on compliance through social networks [1].
3. Institutional Memory: Back Auditing, Detection Efficiency, and Temporal Dynamics

Institutional memory—the ability of tax authorities to review past periods and the degradation of that information over time—emerges as one of the most powerful mechanisms shaping compliance dynamics.

Back auditing as the dominant force. Hokamp [2] explicitly models back auditing (parameter α, the number of previous tax periods subject to audit) in an ABM with four taxpayer types. The results are striking: back auditing dominates income tax evasion dynamics in every simulation scenario. Without back auditing (α = 0), the extent of tax evasion (ETE) is substantially higher and more volatile. With back auditing (α = 10, reflecting the German statute of limitations), tax evasion is dramatically reduced—from 32.5% in period 1 to just 2.5% by period 11 under the simulated parameter set. The author concludes that "back auditing seems to have by far the strongest impact on the dynamics of fiscal fraud" [2].

How back auditing changes taxpayer behavior. Hokamp and Pickhardt [3] provide the theoretical foundation: with back auditing, maximizing a-type agents must consider multiple periods in their utility function. The necessary condition for an inner solution changes substantially (incorporating a summation over α periods), and the upper bound of the audit probability interval becomes individualized. This means that taxpayers who have evaded in the past face a higher effective expected penalty even if current-period audit probability is low, fundamentally altering the economics of the evasion decision [3].

Information decay and the diminishing impact of audits. DeBacker, Heim, Tran, and Yuskavage [8] provide compelling empirical evidence from IRS random audits (the National Research Program, 2006–2009) on the decay of audit effects over time. They find that an audit increases reported taxable income by approximately $1,185 per year on average, but this effect decays differently by income source. For schedule C (self-employment) income—which has little third-party reporting—the audit effect is large (+14.2%) but transitory: the effect in the fifth year after audit is only one-third of the first-year effect. In contrast, for wage income (subject to third-party reporting), the effect is smaller (+1.3%) but more persistent, continuing to increase for 2–3 years [8].

Income volatility as a moderator of institutional memory. DeBacker et al. [8] also show that higher income volatility accelerates the rate at which audit information becomes outdated. Taxpayers with volatile incomes can more easily exploit the information gap created by the passage of time, because the tax authority cannot distinguish between genuine changes in economic circumstances and renewed evasion. This finding directly supports the degradation-of-detection-efficiency mechanism in your model [8].

Differential effects of audit outcomes. Beer, Kasper, Kirchler, and Erard [9] study operational (not random) audits of US self-employed taxpayers and find that the deterrent effect depends critically on the audit outcome. Taxpayers who received an additional tax assessment ("determined noncompliant") reported approximately 64% more income in the year after audit and 44% more after 3 years. However, taxpayers who received no additional tax assessment ("determined compliant") reported 15% less income the year after and 21% less after 3 years—a significant counter-deterrent effect. This suggests that the "institutional memory" of a clean audit can paradoxically reduce future compliance, perhaps by lowering perceived audit risk or eroding tax morale [9].
4. Bounded Rationality and Adaptive Behavior

A unifying theme across the literature is that taxpayers are boundedly rational: they form subjective beliefs about audit probabilities, update these beliefs based on personal experience and social information, and adjust their behavior adaptively over time.

Subjective belief formation. Hashimzade et al. [5–7] model taxpayers who do not know the objective audit probability but form subjective beliefs through two updating mechanisms: (1) the "target effect" (being audited raises subjective probability to P, which then decays by factor δ each period) and (2) social learning from network contacts. Under the target effect (supported by empirical evidence from Advani et al., who find compliance increases post-audit and remains elevated for several periods), subjective beliefs among self-employed taxpayers are sustained at around 0.18–0.20, well above the objective audit probability of 0.05 [5,7].

Social custom updating. Hashimzade et al. [7] also model the updating of the social custom weight χ (the importance attached to honest tax payment). When a taxpayer meets a compliant contact, χ increases; when meeting an evader, χ decreases. This creates endogenous evolution of tax morale within social networks, with different occupational groups developing systematically different attitudes toward compliance [7]. Alm et al. [10] confirm experimentally that this mechanism is operational: individuals converge toward the social norm they perceive through information about their peers.

Age effects and norm evolution. Hokamp [2] incorporates age heterogeneity and social norm updating: older agents have higher risk aversion parameters (increasing in a stepwise fashion every 10 years), modeling the evolution of social norms over the life cycle. Results show a substantial vertical downward shift in tax evasion when age-based norm updating is active, confirming that social memory operates at both individual and cohort levels [2].
5. Synthesis: How the Three Mechanisms Interact

Based on the reviewed literature, we can identify key interactions between the three mechanisms:

    Social memory × Audit strategies: The effectiveness of audit strategies depends on network structure. Risk-based targeting performs better than random auditing, but its success depends on how information about audits propagates through social networks [1,5]. When highly connected individuals are audited, the deterrence effect spreads more widely through social learning [1].

    Institutional memory × Audit strategies: Back auditing fundamentally alters taxpayer behavior by extending the effective penalty horizon [2,3]. However, the decay of detection efficiency over time (due to income volatility and information outdating) creates windows of opportunity for renewed evasion [8]. This suggests that optimal audit strategies must dynamically combine current-period risk targeting with periodic reviews of past returns.

    Social memory × Institutional memory: The social transmission of audit experiences creates a collective institutional memory that shapes aggregate compliance. When taxpayers observe peers being audited and penalized (or not), they update their subjective beliefs and social custom weights accordingly [5,7,10]. The bomb-crater vs. target effect debate highlights that the interpretation of audit experiences—whether they signal increased or decreased future risk—is mediated by social learning.

    The dominance of institutional memory: Across simulations, back auditing consistently emerges as the single most powerful mechanism for reducing tax evasion [2,3]. However, its effects are modulated by social norms and peer effects: even with long lookback windows, if social norms of evasion are entrenched in a network, compliance can remain low [1,4].

Conclusions

Agent-based models of tax evasion reveal that compliance is an emergent property of the dynamic interaction between adaptive taxpayers, enforcement institutions, and social structures. Social memory (learning from neighbors' audit outcomes) creates path dependence and multiple equilibria. Audit strategies (random, risk-based, or income-targeted) determine the pattern of enforcement signals that propagate through social networks. Institutional memory (retrospective auditing and the decay of detection information) fundamentally alters the intertemporal calculus of evasion. The most effective policy interventions combine all three: long back-audit periods to increase the expected cost of evasion, risk-based targeting informed by predictive analytics to focus limited audit resources, and communication strategies that leverage social networks to propagate norms of compliance.
